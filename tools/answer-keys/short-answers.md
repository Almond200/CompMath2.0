# Official short-answer keys

`js/data/problems/answer-keys-short.js` contains answers from the organizers’ posted keys and solutions. The `sources` map records the solution URL for each paper. The paper ID is the problem slug without its final problem number. The import matches numbered questions in the source PDFs to the app’s slugs and checks against local statements. Plain numeric answers can be extracted from the labeled answer in a PDF. Stacked fractions, radicals, and questions with more than one answer need particular care because PDF text extraction can drop signs, fraction bars, and exponents.

Each entry contains an `answers` array of fully correct submissions. Its default format is a single real value. Use `format: "set"` for an unordered list of values, `"sequence"` for a list that must follow a specified order, `"tuple"` for one parenthesized ordered tuple, and `"tuple-set"` for an unordered list of ordered tuples. Put a complete list in one string, such as `["4, 6"]`. For a prompt asking for any one acceptable answer, use a separate array entry for each accepted alternative. Keep the multiple-choice imports in their own files.

Use plain math expressions in the keys. The answer field accepts fractions, `sqrt(3)`, `cbrt(2)`, `pi`, powers, factorials, binomial coefficients such as `choose(2019,3)`, `ln`, and the `sin`, `cos`, and `tan` functions. Separate entries with a comma. Spaces are optional. Exact rational keys require exact equality. The checker reduces fractions, proves common root and trigonometric identities, and cancels equal terms and nonzero factors before evaluation. A small numerical tolerance still applies to keys that evaluate numerically. Questions with an unverified key or an unsupported answer form stay available for self-check. Saved self-check sets keep their existing marks.

Set `external: true` when the problem is read from the original paper and the app has a verified key. Practice links to the paper and grades the response from the key.

Use `"percent"` when the official answer is expressed as a percentage. The player can include or omit the percent symbol. Use `"text"` for short answers such as a day of the week or digits in a specified base. List acceptable forms explicitly. Text matching ignores case and extra spaces.

Use `"complex"` for one complex number and `"complex-set"` for all complex numbers. The player can use `i`, common arithmetic operations, integer powers, and `sqrt(z)` for the principal square root. Integer powers of `i` use its four-value cycle. Other complex powers accept integer exponents from −64 to 64. Real and imaginary components are compared separately. For example, `0, ±i*sqrt(6)` stands for all three values in 2024 HMMT February Algebra and Number Theory #9.

Use `fib(n)` for an exact Fibonacci number with \(F_0=0\) and \(F_1=1\). A key can set `notation: "fibonacci"` to accept `F_n` in the context of that problem. The published answer to 2020 CMIMC Combinatorics and Computer Science #8 can be entered as `(100, F_104+1223)` or `(100, fib(104)+1223)`. [Official solutions](https://github.com/howard36/cmimc-official/blob/master/public/assets/docs/past-tests/2020_CCS_S.pdf)

Use `"interval"` for a real interval or union. The checker accepts bracket notation and inequalities such as `[38,88)` and `38 <= x < 88`. An unbounded endpoint can use `inf` or `infinity`. Separate disjoint intervals with `U` or `or`. Open and closed endpoints affect the grade.

Read the submission instruction in the problem and inspect the typeset official answer when the PDF extraction is ambiguous. Check what a subscript denotes before selecting a format. A Fibonacci number, a digit string in a specified base, and an indexed variable require different handling. Record any notation that still needs a verifier with the problem number and source.

If a key and its solution disagree, first match the question to the original sitting. Check the PDF, any organizer correction, and an independent calculation before changing the accepted value. Record the published and corrected answers with the source. Log unusually large exact values along with the relevant limits of the grader so their support can be decided from the actual problem.

### Source discrepancies

- **2025 CMM Individual #10** grades **6**. The organizers’ published solution says they accepted 7 at the event. Two of the listed cuts produce the same rectangle because `2024 × 44 = 45 × 46`, and another case reproduces the original rectangle. [CMM solutions](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_b0f218885a5445a382b1394779ce59da.pdf)
- **2018 Lehigh #1** grades **20**. The printed answer heading is correct. The final sentence in the worked solution gives 400, which is the square of the requested leg length. The original question asks for the length, and `sqrt(29² − 21²) = 20`. [Lehigh problem and solution](https://www.lehigh.edu/~dmd1/hstest18.pdf)
- **2017 MATHCOUNTS National Sprint #25** accepts **10**, **√193/2**, or **√457/2**. The key says the contest accepted all three side assignments. Apollonius’s theorem gives these three median lengths when BC is 10, 13, or 9 units, respectively. [Official key](https://drive.google.com/file/d/0BxY_uj2XLv-iVXRRaXBTaTNMX28/view)
- **2012 MATHCOUNTS National Sprint #22** stays on self-check. The official key lists 12 units and says a second answer was accepted without identifying it. The diagram puts P between L and O, so the radius is 10 and PN is 12. With P at the origin and O at (0, 2), PK = 9 forces the vertical coordinate of K to be −15/4 by the circle equation. NK = 18 instead forces it to be −33/8. [Problem paper](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1lLn6yCKldgrxUz6SMDPktawlNtN1Cloq/view) · [Official key](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1aVRCZ38ZLhvrwfbZRZcSKYO_KLuozn4U/view)
- **2013 Math Prize for Girls #10** accepts **239** and **236**. The organizers accepted the second count for solutions that treated the edge of the illustrated grid as a boundary. [Official solutions](https://mathprize.atfoundation.org/resources/past-tests/2013/mathprize2013solutions.pdf)
- **2013 CHMMC Individual #13 and #15** use the questions printed in the organizer's problem paper. The solutions booklet gives different questions at those numbers. For #13, the repeating tail \(t=9+1/(5+1/t)\) gives \(y=(21\sqrt{5}+27)/82\), so the usual positive denominator yields 135. The prompt leaves the denominator's sign open, and reversing the signs yields −125. For #15, direct evaluation gives \(133^5+110^5+84^5+27^5=144^5\). [Problem paper](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_f3c07b103e484bfaa5bd8860a763e333.pdf) · [Solutions booklet](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_9fb6b947158c439d8a80c083697a0d53.pdf)
- **2014 CHMMC Individual #3** grades **19** as the group size. The worked solution selects 19 groups of 106 after assuming the winner must win a majority of groups. A strict plurality suffices under the printed rules. For each equal group size \(m\), a win costs \(\lceil 2m/3\rceil\) votes, a loss can cost zero, and a tied group costs \(m-\lceil 2m/3\rceil+1\) when a tie is possible. Comparing the eight divisors of 2014 gives the minimum of 696 votes with 106 groups of 19, using 53 wins, 52 losses, and one tie. [Problem paper](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_63a21695ca7b44ccaf0a20cea326a1f7.pdf) · [Official solutions](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_4e087105c60445aabc2913f6f5759508.pdf)
- **2014 CHMMC Individual #5** accepts **4π** and **2π**, as listed by the organizers. **2015 CHMMC Individual #10** accepts **6** and **5**, depending on whether a single pile counts as a split. [2014 solutions](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_4e087105c60445aabc2913f6f5759508.pdf) · [2015 solutions](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_c129634d052f4c138b7f094e8ae6bbfd.pdf)
- **2019 Calgary Junior Math Contest A8** stays on self-check. The official answer box gives 36 and 156, but any integer satisfying the three primality conditions is valid. For example, 540 also works because 541, 271, and 181 are prime. [Official solutions](https://science.ucalgary.ca/sites/default/files/teams/7/Junior%20Math%20Contest/Jr-Math-examenglish-2019-sol-v2.pdf)
- **2019 Duke Math Meet Individual #9** stays on self-check. The key uses a particular weighting of shot sequences, while the problem provides hit rates and leaves the player's shot selection probabilities unspecified. [Official solutions](https://sites.math.duke.edu/dumu/mathmeet/prob/DMM_2019_Solutions.pdf)
- **2012 BmMT Individual #1** grades **4/9**. The solution gives the original line's slope, −9/4, while the question asks for the perpendicular line's slope. [Official solutions](https://berkeley.mt/archives/bmmt-2012/IF2012S.pdf)
- **2013 BMT Geometry #1** grades **26**. The published solution gives \(a+b=13\), and the prompt asks for perimeter, which is \(2(a+b)\). [Official solutions](https://berkeley.mt/archives/bmt-2013/GeoS2013S.pdf)
- **2007 HMMT February Algebra #6** grades the union \(r<49/27\) or \(r>3\). The two critical values are the local minimum and maximum of the cubic. A nonreal conjugate pair exists when the horizontal line is outside that range. Both boundary levels give only real roots. [Official solutions](https://hmmt-archive.s3.amazonaws.com/tournaments/2007/feb/alg/solutions.pdf)
- **2015 BMT Analysis #4** stays on self-check. The printed recurrence starts at \(n=3\), which leaves \(a_3\) and \(a_4\) free. The stated sum then depends on \(a_4\). [Problem paper](https://berkeley.mt/archives/bmt-2015/AnaS2015.pdf) · [Official solutions](https://berkeley.mt/archives/bmt-2015/AnaS2015S.pdf)
- **2015 BMT Analysis #9** grades **0** for the problem as displayed. The solution booklet identifies the published \(1/n^3\) factor as a typo. With that factor the sum is bounded by \(1/n\), so the limit is zero. The solution booklet's intended factor \(1/n^2\) gives \(\pi/4\). [Problem paper](https://berkeley.mt/archives/bmt-2015/AnaS2015.pdf) · [Official solutions](https://berkeley.mt/archives/bmt-2015/AnaS2015S.pdf)
- **2023 MBMT Lower Number Theory #5** stays on self-check. The official count of 17 assumes positive integers. The printed problem says integers less than 100, so it also includes infinitely many negative odd multiples of 3. [Official solutions](https://mbmt.mbhs.edu/archive/2023/bernoulli/nt/solutions.pdf)

### Large exact values

- **2025 Math Prize for Girls #16** uses `2*3^299`, a 143-digit integer. The checker compares the exact value, so it accepts equivalent expressions such as `6*3^298` and rejects even a tiny rational change to the value. [Official solutions](https://mathprize.atfoundation.org/resources/past-tests/2025/mathprize2025solutions.pdf)
- **2019 CHMMC Individual #12, #13 and #15** have several-hundred-digit integer answers. The checked-in keys keep the closed forms `2^2021-6`, `4*3^1000-5*2^1000+1002`, and `2^2018*3^2` for exact grading. [Official solutions](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_51e4bf40e50b4aa5bd2a5fbc06da8009.pdf)
- **2012 Fall CHMMC Individual #2** stays on self-check. The organizer's example uses the 2013 positive multiples `2013!*k` for `k` from 1 to 2013. The question accepts any valid construction, so a finite list of answers would fail to grade the problem fairly. [Official solutions](https://www.caltechmathmeet.org/_files/ugd/006423_6d6ab3b10a9d4a4fa338828e160aad6d.pdf)
- **2018 HMMT February Combinatorics #7**, **2019 HMMT February Algebra and Number Theory #8**, and **2026 HMMT November Theme #4** use factorials as large as `4038!` in the published expressions. Practice evaluates factorials up to `4096!` with integer arithmetic and bounds the size of intermediate results. For example, both `1013*2026!` and `1013*2027!/2027` are accepted for the last of these problems. [2018 solutions](https://hmmt-archive.s3.amazonaws.com/tournaments/2018/feb/comb/solutions.pdf) · [2019 solutions](https://hmmt-archive.s3.amazonaws.com/tournaments/2019/feb/algnt/solutions.pdf) · [2026 solutions](https://hmmt-archive.s3.amazonaws.com/tournaments/2026/nov/theme/solutions.pdf)
- **2018 HMMT February Algebra and Number Theory #8** stays on self-check. Even in the compact form `(2^2019-1)^2018`, evaluating the official answer would require expanding an integer with more than four million bits. [Official solutions](https://hmmt-archive.s3.amazonaws.com/tournaments/2018/feb/algnt/solutions.pdf)

## Pilot paper

In Practice → Practice a contest → Past contest, search for `2025 HMMT February Algebra and Number Theory`. All ten questions have keys. Question 7 lets you try equivalent expressions involving a radical. Question 10 lets you try listing both values in either order. The Math keypad is optional, and the preview shows how the app parsed your expression. This pilot is untimed and reports the number of correct answers.
